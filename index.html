Vivek Khimani

Software engineer at Semgrep · San Francisco Bay Area

I found my way to computer science through mathematics.

What I liked about math was the idea that something complicated could be built from a small number of simple things. You start with a few primitives, understand them deeply, and build from there. The complexity is still there, but it becomes something you can reason about.

That way of thinking has stayed with me. When I build software or a product, I'm usually looking for the underlying pieces: what actually matters, what can be separated, and what relationships need to exist between them. Get those right, and a lot of the complexity takes care of itself.

Computer science felt like a natural place to put that way of thinking to work. I liked the point where an abstract idea stops being an idea and starts doing something in the real world.

I started my career working on privacy-preserving machine learning, then joined Semgrep, a company I had first encountered in college while using it to grade data structures assignments. At Semgrep, I got to build products from scratch and watch them make their way into the hands of customers. I also learned that building something useful goes well beyond the software itself: understanding the customer, shaping the product, and figuring out how all the pieces of a business fit together. Eventually, I wanted to build something of my own.

I grew up watching my dad run a small agricultural business. It was a good business, but its economics were fairly intuitive: more people, more land, more resources, more output. Software offered a different kind of leverage. You could build something once and have it quietly do useful work for thousands of people. I've always been interested in that contrast, and especially in what happens when that kind of leverage is brought to industries that still operate largely in the traditional way.

A lot of the same thinking shows up in how I approach sport. I grew up playing competitive squash, and these days I play tennis and run. What I like about sport is the repetition. You break something down, pay attention to what is slightly off, and do it again. Eventually, something that once required conscious effort becomes natural. There is something satisfying about getting a little better through focus and repetition.

Outside of work and sport, I like traveling on my own, reading, cooking, and playing guitar. I like being somewhere unfamiliar and figuring things out as I go. And I like having a few things in my life that don't need to lead anywhere.
