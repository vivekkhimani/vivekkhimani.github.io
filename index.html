Vivek Khimani

Software engineer at Semgrep · San Francisco Bay Area

I found my way to computer science through mathematics.

What I liked about math was that complicated things could often be built from a small set of primitives. You could understand those pieces deeply, understand the rules connecting them, and build from there. The trick wasn't eliminating complexity, but finding the right primitives from which it could be built.

That way of thinking has stayed with me. When I build software or a product, I find myself looking for the primitives: what is the system really made of, what actually matters, what should be kept separate, and how should the pieces relate to one another? Get those foundations right, and much of the complexity that follows becomes easier to reason about.

I started my career working on privacy-preserving machine learning, then joined Semgrep, a company I had first encountered in college while using it to grade data structures assignments. There, I got to build products from scratch and watch them make their way into the hands of customers. It taught me that building something useful has its own set of primitives: understand the problem, understand the person experiencing it, decide what matters, and build around those things. The software is only a small part of the system.

I grew up watching my dad run a small agricultural business. Its economics were fairly intuitive: more people, more land, more resources, more output. Software offered a different kind of leverage. You could build something once and have it quietly do useful work for thousands of people. I've always been curious about the intersection of those two worlds: the leverage of software and the tangible, often less digitized industries where it can be applied.

I think some of the same instinct is behind why I like sport. I grew up playing competitive squash, and these days I play tennis and run. Sport is another place where complexity becomes manageable by breaking it down. You isolate a movement, repeat it, notice what is slightly off, and try again. Over time, the pieces become instinctive. I like that process of taking something apart and putting it back together a little better.

Outside of work and sport, I like traveling on my own, reading, cooking, and playing guitar. I like being somewhere unfamiliar and figuring things out as I go. And I like having a few things in my life that don't need to lead anywhere.
