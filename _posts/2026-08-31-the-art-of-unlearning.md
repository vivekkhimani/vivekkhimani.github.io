---
title: The Art of Unlearning
date: 2026-08-31
summary: On muscle memory, and the part of growth that looks like subtraction.
---

I moved out of my house when I was 18.

It was the first time I went to school in the United States, miles away from home
and from almost everything that had felt familiar until then. New people, new
customs, a different way of speaking, a different way of doing things. I was
learning constantly.

At least, that was how I thought about it.

For a long time, I had a pretty simple relationship with learning. I liked
getting better at things. I grew up playing sports, where improvement is almost
tangible. You do the drill, make the mistake, do it again. You watch someone
better than you, try to understand what they are doing differently, and then go
back and practice it until your body starts doing it without having to think.

I carried that instinct into everything else. I like asking questions. I like
taking things apart. I like following an idea all the way down until I understand
why something works, or why it has to be the way it is. There is something
satisfying about taking a thing that initially feels complicated and reducing it
to a few simple principles.

And so I became pretty good at learning. Or at least, I became pretty good at
thinking about myself as someone who should always be learning.

I see this instinct everywhere now, especially among people around my age. We
talk about learning quickly, moving quickly, becoming better versions of
ourselves. There is always another skill to pick up, another book to read,
another habit to build, another weakness to fix.

I love that. I still do. But over the past few years, I've started to notice
something that feels almost like the inverse of learning.

<p class="turn" markdown="1">
<span>Sometimes, the harder thing is not learning something new.</span>
<span>It is letting go of something you already learned.</span>
</p>

---

When I moved to the US at 18, I learned a lot.

But I also had to unlearn a lot.

I had to unlearn interrupting people in certain social situations. I had to
unlearn calling someone randomly instead of texting first. I had to unlearn
things I could say or do at home that simply didn't carry the same meaning
somewhere else.

None of these things felt like learning at the time. There was no syllabus. No
deliberate practice. I just slowly noticed that something I had always done no
longer worked the same way. And then, over time, I stopped doing it.

That process has continued in much quieter ways. For most of my teenage years and
college, sleep was something I negotiated with rather than something I respected.
There was always something else I could do. Another hour of work. Another thing
to finish. Another way to squeeze a little more out of the day.

At some point, I started trying to unlearn that.

<p class="stanza" markdown="1">
<span>To go to sleep on time.</span>
<span>To let a day end without feeling like I had failed to extract everything possible from it.</span>
<span>To occasionally take an afternoon off without turning it into a calculation of whether I had earned it.</span>
</p>

These things sound almost embarrassingly simple when written down. But I've found
that simple doesn't mean easy. Because the difficult part isn't knowing what the
better behavior is.

The difficult part is undoing the part of you that has gotten very good at doing
the old thing.

---

I think about this a lot through sports. In squash and tennis, there are few
things more frustrating than trying to fix a bad habit you've already spent years
building. A bad swing. The wrong preparation. A foot that lands slightly too
late. A tendency to reach for a ball instead of moving your feet.

When you're first learning, you can just learn the right thing. But when you've
already learned the wrong thing, you're fighting something else: muscle memory.
You can know exactly what you are supposed to do and still watch yourself do the
old thing.

<p class="stanza" markdown="1">
<span>So you drill it.</span>
<span>Again.</span>
<span>And again.</span>
<span>And again.</span>
</p>

Ten thousand repetitions of the right movement don't immediately erase the old
one. Sometimes you have to consciously stop yourself from doing what feels
natural. You have to make the new movement feel awkward long enough that,
eventually, it becomes less awkward than the old one.

And even then, under pressure, you sometimes fall back into the old motion. That
has always been one of the more humbling parts of playing sports.

You can know better and still do the wrong thing.

Maybe life works a little like that too.

---

I used to think of growth mostly as accumulation. Learn more. Get better. Add
another skill. Build another habit. Understand another idea.

I'm starting to think some of it is subtraction. There are things I've learned
that I don't necessarily want to keep. Ways of thinking. Ways of working.
Expectations I put on myself. Little behaviors that made sense at one point and
don't anymore.

And unlearning them doesn't feel like learning. It feels slower. Less measurable.
There is no satisfying moment where you can say, *I have now successfully
unlearned this.*

Most of the time, you only notice it in retrospect.

<p class="stanza" markdown="1">
<span>One day you realize you went to bed early without feeling guilty about it.</span>
<span>You took a day off and didn't spend the whole day trying to justify it.</span>
<span>You didn't react the way you used to.</span>
<span>You caught yourself doing the old thing, stopped, and tried something different.</span>
</p>

<p class="stanza" markdown="1">
<span>Another rep.</span>
<span>Then another.</span>
</p>

---

I think that's what makes the idea comforting to me. When I'm frustrated with
myself for not having something figured out, I sometimes think about trying to fix
a bad shot.

I know how long that takes. I know that knowing the right thing isn't the same as
being able to do it. I know that progress can look like doing the same drill badly
for a very long time. I know that sometimes you make something worse before you
make it better, because you have to take apart a movement that once felt
completely natural.

So maybe I can give myself some of that same patience.

<p class="stanza" markdown="1">
<span>It's okay if I don't hit the gym every day.</span>
<span>It's okay if I don't sleep perfectly every night.</span>
<span>It's okay if some days are stressful, unproductive, or just strange.</span>
</p>

None of those things need to become another thing to optimize.

I'm still learning. And, perhaps more importantly, I'm still unlearning.
